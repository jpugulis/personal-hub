Võrtsjärv Expedition

Riders

The first expedition set the template for everything that followed, and it did so almost entirely by accident.

Seven riders took the train to Valka: Jānis Pūgulis, Reinis Tihovs, Toms Romanovskis, Mikus Geidāns, Toms Ziemelis, Dāvis Dreika and Toms Ģērmanis. The WhatsApp group had been created exactly one day before departure. The plan was three relaxed days around Võrtsjärv, Estonia's second-largest lake. What actually happened was a heavy first-night party, a legendary bad bicycle, a thunderstorm crossing the water, and a 213-kilometre ride home that nobody had planned.

Dāvis and the rental

Dāvis Dreika completed the entire tour on a rented bicycle the group later described as an īres miskaste — wheels that barely rolled, suspension rusted solid, every kilometre made harder than it needed to be. He finished anyway, and became the example the group has used ever since to reassure newcomers that a perfect gravel bike is not the price of entry.

It also became the first entry in a pattern: three of the four expeditions have had one rider whose equipment materially changed their experience of the trip.

The rhythm nobody planned

Days 2 and 3 were short — 43 and 59 kilometres — and each occupied close to six hours. Less than half the elapsed time was spent moving. This was partly recovery from the first evening and partly the lake doing its work: swimming, eating, photographing, talking.

It established the group's defining characteristic, visible in every year since: roughly half of every expedition is spent stopped. It is not slack in the schedule. It is where the trip happens.

The lightning

The final evening became the strongest shared memory of the whole four years. The group had rented a cabin, but some riders — Toms Romanovskis and Mikus Geidāns in particular — still intended to sleep outside in hammocks and tarps.

They spent the evening drinking near the pier, watching a thunderstorm approach across the lake from the direction of Latvia. They followed it on lightning radar while watching the flashes in real time over open water, waiting to see whether it would actually reach them.

It did. The wind arrived, then rain that turned nearly horizontal. At least one tarp setup failed completely and its owner retreated, soaked, into the cabin. The place is still referred to in the group's chat as the pier where the lightning was awaited.

Accommodation beside open water can become the expedition itself. What made the storm a memory rather than a problem was having a cabin to retreat into and everyone being together when it hit.

Valka, and then not stopping

Day 4 was supposed to end in Valka. The group reached it after 50 kilometres, around lunchtime, with trains home available.

Jānis, Reinis and Toms Romanovskis kept riding. They added 163 kilometres to Riga, finishing a 213-kilometre day at an average moving speed of 26.2 km/h after three days of touring. Romanovskis went ahead alone from around Ieriķi; the rest of the group followed their progress remotely through Valmiera and Cēsis. Jānis got home around 01:00. He woke in the night with leg cramps and his thighs hurt for days.

It remains the single most important structural lesson in the archive: an optional extension can create a legendary finish without forcing the whole group into it. What made it safe was that Valka was a real exit point with real transport, agreed in advance.

Why it still matters

Almost every principle the group now plans by was visible here first. One base near good water beats moving hotels every night. Short days with long stops beat long days with short ones. Accommodation is part of the experience rather than a bed. The weather can become the memory instead of the problem. And the best moments — the storm, the ride home — were the ones nobody put on an itinerary.

The trip ended with everyone thanking Jānis and immediately discussing the next one. Reinis said he needed to start saving for a gravel bike.
